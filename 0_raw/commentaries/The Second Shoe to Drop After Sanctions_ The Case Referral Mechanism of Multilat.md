# The Second Shoe to Drop After Sanctions: The Case Referral Mechanism of Multilateral Development Banks

Please click "Read more" at the end to find the PDF version of this article.
A Chinese company came under World Bank investigation for sanctionable practices in its overseas bidding. After more than ten months of on-site inquiries, evidentiary exchanges, and settlement negotiations, the company finally reached a negotiated settlement, under which the company accepted debarment, committed to strengthening its compliance program, and submitted to oversight by an independent monitor.
With the settlement agreement signed and the matter seemingly concluded, the legal team turned to preparing for the road to reinstatement. But one easily overlooked clause in the agreement — the Bank's right to refer case information to its member countries — was already, quietly, setting the "second shoe" in motion.
A sanction may cost a company its eligibility to bid on multilateral development bank projects; the referral of case information to member countries may expose it to criminal prosecution. That is the case referral mechanism of multilateral development banks — the second shoe to drop once a sanction takes effect.
01
How Does the World Bank Refer Cases?
Within the World Bank's Integrity Vice Presidency (INT), referral is a distinct track that runs independently of the sanctions process.
Once INT completes an investigation and is satisfied that the evidence substantiates a violation, it first prepares a Final Investigation Report (FIR) for the President of the World Bank Group. On that basis, INT then produces a Referral Report addressed to the governments of the member countries concerned or to other international institutions, such as fellow multilateral development banks.[1]
When does INT make a referral? In short, whenever the evidence indicates that the laws of a World Bank member country may have been violated, or whenever the matter may be of relevance to the operations of another multilateral development bank or international institution.[2]
A referral is more than a way for INT to cooperate with law enforcement elsewhere. It also flows from the fiduciary duty the World Bank owes its member countries as the trustee of international public funds — put simply, the Bank must ensure its funds are not misused, and when it comes across indications of crime, it must tell the member countries concerned.
Over the years, INT has worked to get the most out of the referral mechanism. In 2021 it refined its approach once again, concentrating resources on the cases most likely to produce concrete outcomes — those that stand to benefit the World Bank Group, INT itself, or national investigations.
To keep referred cases from vanishing into a black hole, the Bank built a set of follow-through measures. Back in 2009, the Independent Advisory Board (IAB) had already recommended a graduated response:
Direct engagement: INT approaches the member country's law enforcement authorities directly to advance the investigation;
Diplomatic escalation: if that gets nowhere, the matter can be escalated through diplomatic channels, with the Bank's Country Director raising it with his or her regular counterpart — typically the Minister of Finance;[3]
Public disclosure: in extreme cases, the referral may be made public, though this option must be handled with great care, weighing both law enforcement and political considerations.
Internal oversight: a member country that sits on a referral will find the matter reported to the Bank's Audit Committee and brought to the attention of the Executive Directors. A serious lack of political will can trigger tougher measures — suspension of lending in a given sector or, in the extreme case, suspension of lending to the country altogether.
International cooperation: closer cooperation with the OECD Working Group on Bribery pays off in two ways. If a suspect company has already been convicted by a national court, the Bank can draw on that conviction to shorten its own sanctions proceedings. And once a Notice of Sanctions Proceedings (NOSP) has been filed, referrals routed through the OECD to the home countries of suspected bribers can tap the OECD's own monitoring mechanisms — notably the Tour de Table — to make sure the cases are followed up.[4]
02
A Mixed Reception from National Authorities
The hardest part of making referrals work is getting member governments to move.
Ideally, the moment a country receives a referral it should take prompt action so that the victims of fraud and corruption can obtain redress: criminal investigations, prosecutions and convictions; civil suits to recover assets and the proceeds of crime; administrative measures barring offending companies from government procurement; and proceedings to suspend or dismiss implicated public officials.
In practice, responses vary widely, and the mechanism runs into obstacles at every level.
Albania: After reviewing the evidence passed on by the World Bank, Albania's General Prosecutor concluded that the statute-of-limitations provisions of the country's criminal code barred prosecution of a case originating from a Bank referral. The limitations period closed the case before any investigation could even begin.
Kenya: On receipt of the Bank's Referral Reports, the Kenya Anti-Corruption Commission completed 25 criminal investigations and charged 18 officials. But the courts are severely backlogged, and the cases have dragged on ever since.
China: In 2003, a number of Chinese companies bid on the Philippines National Road Improvement and Management Program (NRIMP-1). On the one hand, INT's investigation found that the companies had breached the Bank's Procurement Guidelines and engaged in sanctionable practices; the Bank halted disbursement of roughly US$33 million under the project and, in 2009, sanctioned four Chinese companies with debarments ranging from five to eight years. On the other hand, some of the companies publicly rejected the findings, and a spokesman for China's Ministry of Commerce said that "according to the Chinese companies concerned, there were defects in the World Bank's evidence and investigative procedures, and their inquiries were not effectively addressed by the World Bank." When the Bank referred the case to China, the authorities closed it without opening a criminal investigation.[5]
03
Persistent Efforts, Growing Results
Faced with this uneven record, INT keeps engaging national authorities to learn why certain referrals went nowhere, so that it can better help them fill gaps in capacity, resources, or legal frameworks.
Canada is a case in point. At first, Canadian authorities could not act on a Bank referral: the Bank's restrictions on how the report could be used collided with Canadian law, under which the origin of an investigation and the underlying complaint may have to be disclosed. Proceedings stalled. Once INT understood the problem, it revised the limiting language in its referral reports, clearing the way for prompt Canadian action. The Bank is now overhauling the relevant provisions across the board, so that recipients facing similar legal constraints can make effective use of its referrals.
Beyond these bilateral efforts, the Bank has invested in multilateral platforms. In 2010 it convened the first meeting of the International Corruption Hunters Alliance (ICHA), which brought together law enforcement practitioners from around the world and has since become an important channel for advancing cross-border prosecutions.[6] INT also works continuously with the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP), the European Anti-Fraud Office (OLAF), and the OECD and its Working Group on Bribery, steadily widening the circle of referral partners.[7]
The effort is paying off. In fiscal 2011, countries where INT had concentrated its outreach delivered encouraging results:
Acting on a Bank referral, Indonesia arrested three individuals — two of them public officials — and audited the project concerned.
Ethiopia's quick follow-through on a referral prevented US$7 million in contracts from going to companies suspected of wrongdoing. In the same year, a referral about an official who had demanded bribes led to the suspect's arrest and prosecution — and the trail led to other entities as well.
Norway's Økokrim (the National Authority for the Investigation and Prosecution of Economic and Environmental Crime), acting on a Bank referral, secured the conviction of three former Norconsult employees over a Bank-financed project in Tanzania.
A referral to the UK's Serious Fraud Office (SFO) ended with Macmillan Publishers paying more than £11 million for its unlawful conduct in an education project in Southern Sudan.[8]
Conclusion
A company once told the authors that it saw multilateral development bank sanctions as "a big headache in a small circle," vowing: "Compliance is such a hassle that we're done with MDB projects — the five multilateral banks don't add up to much business anyway." Years on, the authors have yet to see many companies actually manage to stay away from MDB-financed work.
And the referral mechanism is precisely what shatters that "small-circle" illusion. Multilateral development bank sanctions are not a closed loop among five institutions; they are a node — a hub — connected to the global anti-corruption enforcement network.
To be sure, not every violation is sanctioned, not every sanctioned case is referred, and not every referral triggers criminal action at the national level. But bid documents submitted without proper compliance review years ago are like a boomerang flung carelessly into the distance: you assume it landed where no one would ever notice, until years later it comes flying back from an unexpected direction — and strikes you right between the eyes.
As Chinese companies extend their reach overseas, as multilateral development banks and member countries share information ever more deeply, and as some countries shift their enforcement posture toward China, the odds of that boomerang finding its target keep rising. It is time to rethink the notion of MDB sanctions as "a big headache in a small circle." Where the boomerang thrown years ago lands today, and what damage it does, is no longer the thrower's call.
